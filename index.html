How this works

A cadence sets the key, a chord from the key plays, then a single note rings on top. Name that top note's number, counted from the key's 1, whichever chord is under it. The same note rests over one chord and pulls over another; that's what soloing feels like.

    Hint

    Which chord: a major-sounding chord is 1, 4 or 5; a minor one is 2m, 3m or 6m. Top note: the highest note is the one you'd sing as a melody, and it's what the ear hears most. Bass note: the lowest note colours the chord; with the 3rd or 5th in the bass it feels less settled. The teal dots on the neck are home (1).

    Which numbers make which chord

    Every chord in the key is built the same way: start on a number, skip one, take the next, skip one, take the next. Tap a chord to hear it and see it on the map and neck.

    major 1, 4, 5: a big gap (4 half steps), then a small one (3). Bright, stable.

    minor 2, 3, 6: small gap first (3), then big (4). Darker. 6m is home in minor keys.

    diminished 7: two small gaps (3 + 3). Its top note is squeezed by both half steps of the map (3–4 and 7–1), so it sounds tense and wants to resolve. It's really the 5 chord without its root (5 7 2 4), so it pulls to 1.

    Why they differ: the map's two half steps (3–4 and 7–1) land in different places depending on where you start stacking. The small numbers between dots count half steps.

    Options and tips
    Chords

    Outside chords adds the major 3 and 2 chords and ♭7, in patterns where they resolve the way songs use them. Jazz 7ths uses four-note chords in classic patterns like 2m7 – 5(7) – 1maj7; length is set by the pattern. Listen to the bass. Home (1, or 6m in minor) feels like rest; 5 leans toward home; 4 feels like stepping away; 6m is the dark cousin of 1. With only 1 4 5 6m, once you know three chords the fourth is often the one not used yet.
